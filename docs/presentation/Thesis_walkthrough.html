Motivation

Same label, possibly different construct

Benchmarks are the main instrument for comparing LLM behaviour, yet they are better understood as artifacts of the institutions that build them than as neutral measures (Raji et al., 2021; Branford & Kraft, 2026). Bias benchmarks are built by industry, academia and affected communities, each with its own idea of what a social bias is (Blodgett et al., 2021).

BBQ, which commercial model evaluations still report, defines nine U.S.-centric dimensions. Community-built benchmarks such as HESEIA and WinoQueer stay close to lived experience but rarely enter industry evaluation loops. This thesis asks whether benchmarks that use the same category names actually measure the same thing.

Research question

Do institutional and community-built bias benchmarks operationalize the same demographic and intersecting constructs at the level of dataset content?

RQ1Internal coherence: does each benchmark's own category form a coherent cluster of content?
RQ2Convergence: do same-labelled categories from different benchmarks land in the same semantic region?
RQ3Origin: does the community-built benchmark cover territory that institutional ones leave out?
?Open question
  • BBQ was built by academics but is adopted by industry. Is "institutional vs. community" the right contrast, or should origin be "where it is used" rather than "who built it"?

Construct

Content validity of social-bias categories

Each benchmark category (gender, race, religion, …) is a systematized version of a contested background concept. Following Jacobs & Wallach (2021) and Wallach et al. (2025), this thesis examines content validity: whether an operationalization captures the substantive nature of the construct it claims to measure.

Measurement instrument

Two instruments: the benchmark, and my pipeline that audits it

Wallach et al. (2024), following Adcock & Collier (2001), separate four levels. A bias benchmark is the instrument at level three, and this thesis examines that level's content. Because the clustering pipeline produces the evidence about the benchmarks, it is itself an instrument and has to be validated in its own right.

Theoretical approach

Intersectional and decolonial feminist lens

Demographic axes are treated as socially constructed classifications, not fixed variables. Gender and sexuality are read through decolonial feminism, as categories imposed through colonization (Lugones, 2010). Intersectionality means axes are co-constituted, not additive (Crenshaw, 1989). Data feminism asks to rethink binaries and hierarchies in data (Klein & D'Ignazio, 2024).

Empirically, this becomes one testable contrast: do a benchmark's intersectional items form their own territory (co-constitutive), or fall inside one parent axis (additive)?

?Open questions
  • Co-occurring labels (HESEIA) or two-axis categories (BBQ's Race×Gender) are a thin operationalization of intersectionality. Is there a better computational handle?
  • How many axes to analyse: all eleven, or the four that every benchmark shares (age, gender, disability, race)?
Methodology and justification

Each step has a precedent and a check

1
Unit of analysis: the template

BBQ's 58,492 items are copies of a few hundred hand-written scenarios, with different names and group labels swapped in. Copies of one scenario are not independent evidence. This thesis therefore counts each scenario once: masked items are reduced to the distinct texts of each template, unmasked items are capped at six per template, and every test shuffles and resamples whole templates, never single items.

The overall design follows computational grounded theory (Nelson, 2020): clusters detect patterns, close reading interprets them, and classifiers plus sensitivity checks confirm them.

How each step works

The pipeline in pseudocode

A conceptual version of what the code does. The formal notation is kept in docs/statistical_tests_full.md for later.

Embedding 
for each item text s:
    e = LaBSE(s)          # 768 numbers; LaBSE is never retrained
    e = e / length(e)     # so dot(e_i, e_j) = cosine similarity

Similar meanings get nearby vectors, in English and Spanish alike.

UMAP 
connect each item to its 15 nearest neighbours
squeeze 768 dimensions into 5,
    keeping neighbours close together
# random start, so the seed is fixed

Makes the space small enough for density-based clustering (McInnes et al., 2018).

HDBSCAN 
find dense regions in the 5-d space
keep a region as a cluster if it
    stays stable across density levels
    and has ≥ min_cluster_size items
items in no dense region → noise (-1)

The number of clusters is not fixed in advance (McInnes & Healy, 2017).

Coherence 
purity     = does each cluster hold one category?   # 0..1
dispersion = does each category stay in one cluster? # 0..1
gap = avg similarity within a category
    - avg similarity across categories

Purity and dispersion are homogeneity and completeness (Rosenberg & Hirschberg, 2007). The gap uses the full space, so it doesn't depend on UMAP or HDBSCAN settings.

Identity probes 
for each dimension d in HESEIA (age, gender, ...):
    train logistic regression:
        embedding → "is this item about d?"
    quality     = AUC on held-out items
    control     = same, with shuffled labels
    selectivity = quality - control

A probe only counts if it beats the shuffled control (Hewitt & Liang, 2019).

Projection 
for each BBQ cluster k:
    score every item with every probe
    profile(k) = average score per dimension
    driver(k)  = dimension with the highest average

Shows which HESEIA dimension, from the community's standpoint, a BBQ cluster is about.

?Open questions
  • Is a linear probe enough to say a dimension is "represented" in the embeddings?
  • Is the average-similarity gap a good enough coherence measure, or does it miss how spread out a category is?

BBQ's weight follows copies, not stereotypes

Substantive validity · data, not yet clustering
share of templatesshare of itemsright: items per template

The authors wrote 25 scenarios for most categories, 50 for gender identity and race/ethnicity (25 with labels, 25 with names). Each scenario was then copied by swapping in different people. Race×Gender holds 27% of all items from 12% of the templates; the two intersectional categories together hold 46% of BBQ. Sexual orientation has as many templates as Age or Religion but only 1.5% of the items. Items per template range from 35 to 429, set by the length of name and label lists, not by the stereotypes.

The BBQ paper explains why: names are a common way race and gender are signalled, and intersectional templates need three kinds of comparison. It gives no reason for the range of copies per template and does not say whether scores weight items or templates. Any score averaged over items therefore leans on race-related intersectional content. This is why this thesis treats the template as the unit of analysis.

No category has more than 23% of its items in one cluster. Relative to their number of templates, Religion and Gender identity are the most compact, and Nationality and Race/ethnicity the most scattered. Identity words carry most of the category signal

RQ1 · linear probe on the nine single-axis categories, tested on unseen scenarios

Grey: tested on copies of scenarios seen in training (memorizing). Blue: tested on unseen scenarios (honest). Chance is about 0.11.

On scenarios it never saw, a simple classifier recognises the category with macro-F1 0.85 when identity words are visible, but 0.42 when they are masked. The scenario content carries some category signal, clearly above chance, but most of it comes from who is named. The gap between seen and unseen scenarios (0.83 vs. 0.42 masked) shows how much a naive evaluation would overstate.

Where categories blur together without identity words

Masked, unseen scenarios. Rows: true category; columns: predicted. The outlined diagonal is correct.

Gender identity is the easiest to recognise from content alone (0.64), disability the hardest (0.13), and its scenarios are read as nationality or sexual orientation. Nationality is often read as race (0.24), and SES as gender identity (0.28). Sexual orientation is sometimes read as gender identity (0.13), rarely the reverse (0.02).

Intersectional items read as race

Structural validity · classifier trained on single-axis categories only
race / ethnicitythe second axis (gender or SES)other

With names visible, 84% of Race×Gender items are classified as race and only 10% as gender; Race×SES items read as race (62%) more than as SES (31%). This supports an additive reading: BBQ's intersectional categories function mostly as race categories with a second attribute attached. A classifier must choose one label, so notebook 02's probes, which can register both axes at once, are the fairer test.
